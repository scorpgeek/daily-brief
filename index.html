10 One Long-Form Read Objection

Not Every Selection is a Conspiracy

Anand Vasu · VITALS · p.10

The argument

Agarkar has departed as chief selector and his successor hunt is underway, so the squad announcement is being read as a signal. Vasu accepts the outrage is real, then argues it is misdirected. The committee selected Kuldeep over Manav Suthar, and returned Anshul Kamboj in place of Aaqib Nabi; Jaiswal is absent from the ODI squad, Gurnoor Brar is held out of the Test side, and Sarfaraz is omitted again.

Each omission, on his reading, is a long-view call: Suthar is better served playing domestic cricket before a five-Test Border-Gavaskar Trophy, Brar is being sharpened for the 2027 World Cup, Jaiswal is playing three four-day matches for India A. Consistency of process, with Gambhir and the captain, is what builds trust — and every feel-good story is backed by exponentially more near misses.

The claim I'm attacking

That the selections are defensible and the outrage is disproportionate.

The joint

Vasu asks you to trust the process because it is consistent. But consistency is not evidence of correctness — a consistently poor process is still a poor process, and internal logic is a property of any argument, including the wrong one. His strongest card is that the omissions have identifiable internal reasoning, and he is right about that. The gap is that he never shows the reasoning discriminates between a genuine long view and a story assembled afterwards.

The argument

Every selector in cricket believes they are playing the long game. That belief is unfalsifiable from outside unless someone specifies it in advance and holds them to it afterwards, and Vasu does neither. He invokes "logic borne out by data" while presenting no data at all — no record of how many previous long-view calls the committee made, how many paid off, or what the base rate was.

Absent that, "the long view" is a phrase doing all the work, and it can be fitted to any omission in hindsight. This is why conspiracy accusations persist. A genuine process invites audit; an unfalsifiable one invites conspiracy, because the outsider has no way to distinguish confidence from rationalisation.

What I would concede

Vasu is substantially right on the merits. Selecting on availability rather than projected role would be worse, and Suthar is genuinely better placed playing first-class cricket than as a tourist in a spin-friendly Test. He is also right that outrage without a counterfactual is cheap. His line about near misses is a fair correction to the assumption that every omission is an injustice.

What would change my mind

A track record. If this committee's last two comparable long-view calls paid off, the process has earned trust retrospectively, even though it could not prove itself in advance. Vasu's argument would then be complete, because trust is exactly what he is claiming and evidence of results is exactly what he is missing.

How this differs

Vasu argues from inside the framing — he accepts the selectors have a coherent long view and defends them on their own terms. My objection accepts his framing completely and shows it does not discriminate: it would produce the same confidence if the committee were wrong.

14 Traps in My English5 errors
"The team will be collectively responsible for the shift" → "I'll own the shift and Priya will own the QA numbers"
Passive voice that names no owner. The most common finding in a performance review, because it can't be repeated back as an action.
"Your mail and mine" → "As I understand it, the escalation window is 24 hours"
Asserts agreement without stating what is agreed.
"There is no last-minute change in the schedule" → "There are no last-minute changes in the schedule"
Subject–verb agreement. "Change" is singular, so the verb is "is".
"Please find attached the compliance report" → "I've attached the compliance report"
A calque from a formal template. Shorter, and sounds like a person wrote it.
"We request you to kindly arrange to send the report by EOD" → "Please send the report by end of day today"
Three hedges in one line. Each dilutes the other and the deadline gets lost.
15 Speak60 sec

Prompt

An error you knew about reached the client before you raised it, and they've asked why they heard it from us. Speak to your team.

Model answer

Alright — straight up, this one's on me. I knew about the mapping error on Wednesday. I didn't raise it because I thought we'd fix it before the report went out. We didn't, and it went out, and that's on me.

Here's what I'm not going to do. I'm not going to get flippant about it, and I'm not going to write a long explanation of why the process failed. The client is the arbiter here, and they decided I should have escalated. That's a fair read.

We also don't get absolution for the fact that it worked out okay. If we'd been wrong about that, we'd be having a different conversation.

What changes from tomorrow: anything that reaches the client gets a 24-hour hold, and I own that list personally. Questions?
16 Quiz5 questions

1. Gandhi opposed separate electorates. An electorate is:

a) voters in an area  ·  b) reserved seats  ·  c) candidates  ·  d) the vote count

2. Which uses flippant correctly?

a) only affected billing  ·  b) gave a flippant answer  ·  c) don't be flippant about attrition  ·  d) flippant tone made it longer

3. Register. Need the client to escalate by 5pm. Which is right?

a) "We request you to kindly escalate at the earliest."  ·  b) "Please escalate the vendor issue by 5pm today."  ·  c) "The issue needs escalation, we think."

4. "Throw good money after bad" means:

a) saving carefully  ·  b) investing more in something already failing  ·  c) paying twice  ·  d) losing unexpectedly

5. "Never seeking absolution." Absolution means:

a) a refused apology  ·  b) forgiveness  ·  c) an excuse  ·  d) a complaint

Answers

1. a — an electorate is the body of voters. (b) is the reserved-seats mechanism Gandhi opposed.

2. c — treating something serious as trivial. (a) and (d) misuse it as a synonym for "informal".

3. b — hedge-free, carries the deadline. (c) withholds the ask entirely, which is worst: the client must guess what's wanted.

4. b — continuing to spend on something already failing, hoping to recover the loss.

5. b — forgiveness. The point is that she never asks the reader to excuse her, which is a different act.
