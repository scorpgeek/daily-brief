06My Lens

What
2,100-plus GCCs now generate $98.4 bn in India employing 2.36 million people, and the work has shifted from cost-focused managed services to owning transformation and product development. PwC's Ojha calls it a "strategic twin." The Big Four's own line: "An engine manufacturer doesn't want to sell engines; it wants to sell power by the hour."
Why me
That is your industry's structural shift, stated plainly. If GCCs become the client's internal capability rather than a buyer of outsourced work, the volume your account commands is exposed.
Do
Ask which of your account's processes are commoditised support and which are the kind a client would build in-house. Talking point: "they're not buying seats anymore, they're buying capability — so let's price the capability, not the headcount."
What
LTM's chief executive says the linkage between revenue and headcount is already broken, with 60% of the portfolio through AI deflation — the same deflation TCS has already sustained at 50%.
Why me
Two of India's largest IT services firms have publicly stated that delivering the same work now takes materially fewer people. Your productivity argument stops being an efficiency gain and becomes a commercial one.
Do
Quantify your own deflation honestly: what did you deliver last quarter with how many people, versus a year ago? If the answer is "the same work, fewer people," that is the argument — and it should be yours before it is anyone else's.
What
The Big Four are fighting over GCC work with specialist operators, talent providers and real-estate players also entering — 150 to 160 active participants.
Why me
Your client has more options than it had two years ago. That is leverage you should know about and use, not be surprised by.
Do
Stop describing your value as effort or headcount. Name the outcome you own, the failure you'd prevent, and what your account would cost to rebuild.

Context — external research

The paper reports the NIM fall as an accounting consequence of expensive deposits. It does not say why those deposits arrived. External sources make the cause explicit: the FCNR(B) inflow was a deliberate RBI intervention to defend the rupee, not a market movement.

The central bank launched a US dollar–rupee forex swap facility on 8 June 2026, absorbing the entire ~3% hedging cost on fresh three-to-five-year deposits and lifting the interest rate ceiling until 30 September. It drew $52.3 bn in FCNR(B) deposits by 14 August and closed the window a month early, on 31 August. Total inflows across FCNR(B), OFCB and ECB channels reached $56.85 bn, lifting forex reserves to a four-month high of $707 bn.

The margin compression was therefore a price of currency defence. Banks were made to hold expensive dollar liabilities to protect the rupee after it touched 96.87 on 20 May.

＋Context — web-sourced

Global capability centres — what the paper omits

The paper reports that the Big Four are competing for GCC work. External reporting shows a larger structural change underneath it: nine in ten new GCCs set up in India now use external partners, a sharp reversal from the do-it-yourself model dominant in the 1990s and 2000s. Around 60–70% are partner-led; 30–40% remain DIY.

For a services firm, that reframes the story. The competition for advisory work is real, but the build-out itself has become the market — and it is being handed to external partners by default.

Two further figures worth carrying: GCC hiring volumes fell 28% between the first and second half of FY26, with companies reskilling rather than recruiting. And 46% of GCCs now sit at what Nasscom and Zinnov call Portfolio or Transformation hub maturity — owning products and global decisions. A new GCC used to reach that level in ten years; it now takes five.

  • Economic Times, 26 Mar 2026 — nine in ten GCCs use external partners (EY, ANSR)
  • Nasscom–Zinnov GCC Landscape Report 2026, May — 2,117 centres, 3,728 units
  • CNBC TV18, 6 May 2026 — hiring down 28% H1→H2 FY26
  • HFS Research / Capgemini, Apr 2026 — 76% of enterprises have India presence
A discrepancy the paper inherits

The paper cites 2,100-plus GCCs in India. That matches Nasscom–Zinnov exactly, so the figure is well sourced. But the Economic Survey 2025–26 counts only 1,700-plus centres employing over 19 lakh people — on the same figures, in the same year.

Both are credible; they count different things. Worth knowing the range before you quote either number in a meeting.

09Opinion: 2-Minute Read

Make Employees Your Customers
An on-demand services platform made its own gig workforce its most reliable revenue stream, selling ₹1.4 cr of mandatory workwear out of ₹12.2 cr operational revenue. The company reported a net loss of about ₹168 cr, and the fastest route to more revenue was onboarding 10,000 more workers and selling them branded T-shirts.
Leaves out
It treats the piece as satire when the mechanics — mandatory purchases deducted from worker earnings — describe a real and litigated category. It stops at ridicule rather than asking what regulation would look like.
Sit with
What does your organisation sell to people who cannot realistically decline?
Get a Tech List to Tick
Abhay Karandikar and Aditya Sinha, NITI Aayog, argue India has every instrument it needs — ANRF by statute, ₹1 lakh crore in RDIF, PLIs across a dozen sectors — but no evidence-based list of which technologies it cannot afford to depend on others for.
Leaves out
It names the right problem and stops one sentence before the hard one. "Being critical is not the same as being winnable" is asserted, then abandoned — no criteria, no decider, no treatment of the case where something critical is not winnable.
